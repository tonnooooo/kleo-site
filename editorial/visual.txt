shot-size|Choose shot sizes that explain the action|A wide view establishes a space, a closer view directs attention and a detail view makes a small action readable. Choose the framing for the information the viewer needs, not simply to vary the pictures.|Start with enough context to identify the place.~Move closer when a decision or object becomes important.~Return to a wider view when the consequence changes the scene.|Describe three shots for a fictional clock repair: a workbench with the clock, the repairer noticing a loose component, then the clock working on the shelf. Explain what each framing reveals. Avoid a detail shot that requires an exact mechanical demonstration.|Is the setting established?~Are important actions large enough to see?~Does a close view reveal something new?|Constant close framing can hide spatial relationships. Review the sequence without narration to see whether the audience can understand where the action occurs.
camera-movement|Give camera movement a narrative purpose|Camera movement should guide attention or change the viewer's relationship to the subject. A restrained movement with a clear destination is easier to evaluate than several simultaneous movement instructions.|Decide what should become more important during the shot.~Choose one camera action that supports that change.~Explain where the camera should end and what must remain visible.|Plan a slow move toward an unopened letter on a desk while the narrator explains why the recipient hesitates. Keep the desk still and avoid combining an orbit, zoom and sudden tilt. Treat the movement as a requested direction, not a guaranteed physical camera simulation.|Does movement reveal or emphasize information?~Is the subject still readable?~Is the motion comfortable to watch?|Complex movement can distract from a simple story and may produce unwanted distortions. Prefer a stable alternative when movement does not carry essential meaning.
character-continuity|Plan character continuity across generated scenes|Character continuity depends on recognizable features that remain stable from shot to shot. A written character description helps establish intent, but the rendered result still needs inspection for changes in appearance.|Write a compact character sheet with age range, hairstyle, clothing and distinguishing features.~Keep the description consistent across the treatment and shot notes.~Review adjacent shots together rather than approving each image alone.|A fictional adult gardener wears a plain blue coat and has short gray hair. Keep that description unchanged across a gate, a greenhouse and a bench scene. If the rendered appearance drifts, flag the sequence for revision rather than explaining the change in narration.|Do face and clothing remain consistent?~Does the character's apparent age change?~Are repeated props recognizable?|Do not advertise perfect identity consistency based on a prompt alone. If likeness is essential, decide in advance what variation is acceptable and when another production approach is needed.
prop-continuity|Keep important props consistent|A prop continuity plan records the object's appearance, location and state at each story beat. It matters most when the audience must recognize the same item or notice that it has changed.|Give the prop a simple visual identifier.~Track who holds it and whether it is open, full, broken or repaired.~Check every cut where its state matters.|Use one plain red notebook in a fictional messenger story. It begins closed on a desk, is carried under the messenger's arm and ends open on a bench. Do not show readable text inside it. List the notebook's expected state beside each scene.|Does the object change color or size?~Does it move without an action?~Is the important change visible?|Minor background variation may be harmless, but a plot-bearing prop changing identity can break the story. Prioritize continuity checks according to narrative importance.
light-and-time|Make lighting and time of day coherent|Lighting choices tell viewers where and when events happen. Keep the overall time and light direction coherent unless the story explicitly moves to a different moment.|Name the time of day and dominant light source.~Describe any intentional time jump in the treatment.~Compare consecutive shots for unexplained changes in daylight or weather.|Plan a 60-second animation set during one rainy afternoon in a small workshop. Use cool window light and a warm desk lamp. The rain continues throughout; the ending changes the character's mood, not the time of day. Avoid an unexplained sunset shot.|Does the apparent time remain stable?~Are major light sources plausible?~Is a time jump clear when intended?|Beautiful individual images can imply different days when cut together. Judge the lighting as a sequence rather than choosing each shot for its isolated appearance.
color-palette|Use a restrained color palette in a film|A small palette can connect separate scenes and direct attention to an important object. Assign colors a purpose rather than listing many hues that compete for attention.|Choose a base palette for the setting.~Reserve one accent for the element the viewer should track.~Check contrast and meaning after rendering instead of relying on color names alone.|Create a written visual plan for a paper-boat story using muted blue surroundings and one warm yellow boat. Keep the background restrained. Do not use color alone to explain which object matters: framing and narration should also identify it.|Is the accent still noticeable?~Do scene colors feel related?~Is information understandable without distinguishing every hue?|A palette instruction does not ensure consistent output. Check the actual frames, and avoid making comprehension depend entirely on subtle color differences.
vertical-composition|Compose a scene for vertical video|Vertical composition needs a clear subject and careful control of side-to-side action. Design the scene for the tall frame from the start rather than assuming a landscape composition will survive a crop.|Identify the essential subject and keep its action readable in a narrow frame.~Reduce competing background elements.~Leave room for any captions or interface overlays added during publishing.|Plan a 9:16 animation of a gardener lifting a small plant onto a shelf. Keep the face, hands and plant visible together. Avoid a wide row of important objects across the frame. Describe a final shot that still reads on a phone.|Is the main action visible without cropping?~Are important details near risky edges?~Has the upload preview been checked?|There is no single safe-area measurement for every app and device. Preview the finished video on the destination platform before committing to text placement.
landscape-composition|Use landscape framing for narrated explainers|Landscape framing can show relationships across a space, but extra width should serve the explanation. Give the viewer a clear path through the image instead of filling every area with detail.|Decide which relationship requires the wider view.~Place the main action where it remains easy to find.~Use separate shots when two processes cannot be understood simultaneously.|Plan a 16:9 fictional workshop explainer showing a broken item on one side of a bench and a repaired version on the other. Use the narrator to guide the comparison. Avoid readable labels inside generated frames; add precise labels later if needed.|Does the wider frame add information?~Is the subject obvious at small size?~Can details be simplified without losing meaning?|A large canvas is not an instruction to introduce more ideas. The narration and picture should still share one dominant point at any given moment.
realistic-or-animation|Choose realistic or animation for your story|Choose the look according to what the viewer must understand and what the images might imply. A realistic generated scene can resemble recorded evidence; animation can communicate a concept without suggesting the event was filmed.|Describe the role of imagery: illustrative, fictional or tied to a documented event.~Compare how each look changes the viewer's expectations.~State the chosen look consistently in the approved brief.|Compare two written directions for a fictional lost-letter story: realistic atmosphere and simplified animation. Discuss which better emphasizes the letter's journey rather than visual spectacle. Do not render either option until I choose one and approve the cost.|Does the look fit the subject?~Could imagery be mistaken for documentation?~Are disclosure needs considered?|Selecting a realistic look does not make a scene authentic footage. Avoid presenting invented visuals as proof that a real event happened exactly as shown.
complex-actions|Simplify actions that are difficult to inspect|A complex action often contains several object interactions and changes of state. Break it into clear beats when accurate depiction matters, and avoid relying on generated footage as a technical demonstration.|Identify the beginning and end state of the action.~Choose the transition the audience truly needs to see.~Replace unnecessary physical detail with a simpler explanatory scene.|Simplify a fictional character assembling a complicated radio into noticing the problem, working at the bench and testing the result. Do not imply this is a step-by-step repair lesson. Keep the radio recognizable and let the narration explain the story's intention.|Are hands and objects plausible?~Is cause and effect clear?~Could viewers mistake illustration for instruction?|A visually plausible result is not proof of a correct technical process. For instructional work, use verified real demonstrations when precise hand movements or mechanisms are essential.
text-in-generated-scenes|Keep critical text out of generated scenery|Names, numbers and instructions need exact spelling. When a film depends on readable text, plan a separate editing step rather than assuming lettering inside a generated scene will be accurate.|Identify every word or number that must be exact.~Keep those details out of the generated background.~Add verified text in an editor and inspect the final export at viewing size.|Make a 30-second fictional library announcement with a clean closing composition. Do not generate a date, URL or address within the scene. Leave a calm area for a verified event title that I will add in an editor after downloading the film.|Are all critical details added accurately?~Is text readable on a phone?~Does it remain long enough to understand?|Text-free generation is a request, not proof that accidental lettering will never appear. Inspect signs, book covers and background objects before publication.
