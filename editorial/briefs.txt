one-question-video|Build a video around one answer|A useful explainer begins with a question that can be answered within the running time. Narrow the question before choosing a visual style: a polished film about everything usually leaves the viewer with nothing specific.|Write the viewer's question in their own words, without a product slogan.~Draft a one-sentence answer; remove any branch that does not support it.~Choose three visible moments that make the answer understandable without adding a second topic.|Plan a 45-second narrated film about why a bicycle needs gears, for someone learning to ride. Explain the practical trade-off, not the history of bicycles. Show a flat road, a hill and a rider changing effort. Use animation and English narration. Return a treatment before rendering.|Can a viewer repeat the answer in one sentence?~Does each scene support that answer?~Have unsupported technical claims been removed?|Avoid promising to explain an entire subject in a Short. If the outline contains five unrelated questions, split the brief before any production credits are spent.
audience-brief|Define the audience for an AI video|An audience brief describes what the viewer already understands, why they are watching and what they need next. Age or job title alone cannot determine the right vocabulary or level of explanation.|Describe the situation in which someone will watch, such as preparing for a first interview.~List two things the audience knows and two things the video must explain.~Choose one action or understanding that should change after viewing.|Create a 60-second English narrated orientation for people attending their first community repair workshop. Explain what to bring and how to ask for help. Do not assume they own tools. Use fictional participants and an animation look; ask me for the workshop's actual rules before writing details.|Are unfamiliar terms explained?~Does the example match the viewer's situation?~Is the requested next step realistic?|Do not ask the assistant to invent audience research or quote conversion rates. Treat the audience description as a working hypothesis until you have feedback from actual viewers.
source-pack|Prepare a source pack for a factual video|A source pack separates approved facts from narrative instructions. Give each important claim a source, a date and wording the narrator may safely use; a confident treatment is not evidence that its claims are true.|Make a claim list containing the precise statement, its source URL and any qualification.~Mark disputes and missing evidence explicitly rather than asking the assistant to fill gaps.~Approve the spoken facts before the storyboard adds visual interpretation.|Use only the three supplied source excerpts to plan a 90-second explainer. For every factual sentence, identify its supporting excerpt in the treatment. If the excerpts disagree, flag the disagreement for me. Do not invent quotations, dates or expert interviews. Wait for approval before rendering.|Can every important factual claim be traced?~Do pictures imply stronger evidence than the narration?~Are sources suitable for the actual claim?|A source link by itself is insufficient when a long page contains several different claims. Save the relevant passage and its context with your production notes.
creative-constraints|Write useful creative constraints|Creative constraints work best as a short list of visible requirements and clear exclusions. They help a director review a treatment; they do not guarantee that a generative renderer will follow every instruction exactly.|Separate must-have story events from preferences such as lighting.~Limit visual requirements to details the audience could actually notice.~Explain which requirement takes priority if two requests conflict.|Plan a 30-second fictional story set inside one bakery before sunrise. One adult baker, one tray and one window are essential. Use a warm animation look, English narration and 9:16 framing. No written signs or other characters. If the short running time makes the ending unclear, simplify the middle.|Are requirements observable in the rendered film?~Are essential facts distinct from stylistic preferences?~Have competing instructions been resolved?|Long lists of negative instructions can obscure the central action. State the scene you want first, then a few exclusions that protect the story.
treatment-approval|Approve a treatment before rendering|A treatment is the short written plan for the film: its premise, angle, opening, development and ending. Approve that structure before paying to render scenes, because a beautiful shot cannot repair an unclear argument.|Ask for a logline and a scene-by-scene explanation of what changes.~Read the ending first and check that the opening sets it up fairly.~Return one consolidated list of changes, then approve the revised treatment and current cost.|Before making the film, give me a treatment for a 60-second story about a lost umbrella returning to its owner. Show the opening image, three turning points and the final image. Explain how the narrator connects them. Do not start a render until I approve the story and quote.|Does the ending answer the opening?~Is every scene necessary?~Is the quoted product a film or an animatic?|Do not confuse agreeing with an idea with approving a charge. State explicitly whether the assistant may plan only or may also start production.
script-to-brief|Turn an existing script into a production brief|Turning a script into a brief means identifying what must be spoken, what can be shown and what cannot be reproduced faithfully. Preserve the intended meaning before compressing the text into a shorter film.|Mark exact wording that must survive and passages that can be paraphrased.~Convert visual descriptions into simple scene actions.~Read the shortened script aloud before approving duration.|Adapt my supplied script into a 90-second, 16:9 English narrated film. Preserve the closing sentence exactly. Suggest cuts separately and show the revised narration before creating the storyboard. Use the realistic look, but do not invent people, quotations or data absent from the script.|Are mandatory lines retained?~Have cuts altered the argument?~Can the narration be spoken naturally in the proposed duration?|Pasting a long article and requesting a short film invites silent omissions. Identify the essential claim and review the proposed cuts explicitly.
reference-look|Describe a visual reference without copying it|A useful look reference identifies lighting, palette, framing and material qualities. Naming a famous film alone leaves the assistant to guess which qualities matter and may encourage unnecessary imitation.|Describe three visual properties instead of requesting an exact replica.~Separate the reference's mood from its characters, logos and distinctive designs.~Choose a sample scene to judge the direction before expanding the idea.|Plan an original animation about a paper boat crossing a puddle. Use soft side lighting, restrained blue and amber colors, and simple textured backgrounds. Keep the story and character design original. Give me two written visual directions before choosing one for a 30-second film.|Can the look be described without a brand name?~Do style choices support the subject?~Is the desired detail visible at mobile size?|A visual reference is guidance, not permission to reproduce someone else's material. Review the rights for any actual assets used outside the generated workflow.
revision-notes|Give revision notes an AI assistant can use|Effective revision notes identify a specific moment, explain the problem and describe the desired change. A request to make everything more cinematic gives no clear basis for deciding whether the next version is better.|Quote the treatment line or scene number that needs work.~Explain the viewer's confusion rather than only naming a disliked style.~Prioritize story corrections before optional visual polish.|Revise scene three of the treatment: the viewer cannot tell why the courier turns back. Add a visible reason, such as the unclaimed parcel, and remove the unrelated street montage. Keep the existing opening and ending. Show the new treatment only; do not rerender yet.|Is every note tied to a scene?~Is the desired change testable?~Does the revision preserve already approved material?|Do not assume an existing render can be edited in place or that another attempt is free. Check the available workflow and cost before commissioning a new version.
client-brief|Collect a client brief for a narrated film|A client brief should establish the message, audience, deliverables, approvals and supplied evidence. It is a production agreement in plain language, not a substitute for the client's factual or brand review.|Ask the client to approve one objective and one call to action.~List required assets and identify anything that needs a separate editor.~Name the person who can approve the treatment and final export.|Prepare a treatment for a 45-second introductory film for a fictional bicycle repair service. Leave business claims and contact details as items for client approval. Use generated illustrative scenes, one English narrator and landscape framing. Flag any step requiring a logo or real interface to be added later.|Who approves factual claims?~Are additional editing tasks assigned?~Is delivery distinct from publication?|Avoid promising an exact product likeness or authentic business footage when the workflow produces illustrative scenes. Establish that distinction before the client approves the concept.
one-call-to-action|Choose one call to action for a short film|A short film's call to action should follow naturally from the information it gives. One clear next step is easier to evaluate than a closing list asking viewers to subscribe, buy, share and contact you simultaneously.|Choose the action that fits the viewer's current level of interest.~Make sure the story supplies a reason to take that action.~Reserve enough time for a calm spoken ending rather than squeezing it into the last instant.|Plan a 30-second animation introducing a fictional neighborhood book exchange. End with one spoken invitation to read the participation guide. Do not invent an address, opening date or signup link. Keep the final visual simple so I can add verified details later in an editor.|Is there one main action?~Does the viewer know why it is useful?~Are destination details verified before upload?|A call to action is not a performance guarantee. Test whether people understand the invitation before judging whether a different visual effect is needed.
brief-version-control|Keep a clear history of video briefs|A brief history records which story, narration and constraints produced each result. Without version labels, feedback can refer to one draft while the assistant renders another.|Give each approved brief a short version name and date.~Keep the treatment, quote and job identifier together.~Record why a revision was made so a later collaborator can understand the choice.|Label this approved treatment harbor-story-v03. Summarize the changes from v02: shorter opening and a clearer final action. Before rendering, repeat the duration, aspect ratio, narration language, look and cost attached to v03. Do not reuse an earlier treatment silently.|Can the final file be traced to a brief?~Are rejected versions clearly marked?~Is the latest approval easy to find?|Avoid using file names such as final-final-new as your only record. A small decision log is more useful than a folder full of unexplained exports.
