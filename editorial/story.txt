opening-hook|Write an opening that earns the next scene|An opening hook creates a specific question the film will answer. It can be an unexpected action, a visible problem or a clear contrast; it does not require a sensational claim or a promise the story cannot keep.|Write the question raised by the first image.~Check that the treatment answers it before the end.~Remove any introductory explanation viewers do not need yet.|Open a 45-second fictional film with a lighthouse keeper placing a lamp in a window despite the lighthouse already shining. Build the story around why the smaller light matters. Use one narrator and avoid introducing an unrelated mystery in the middle.|Is the question understandable immediately?~Does the answer arrive?~Does the hook accurately represent the film?|Withholding every useful detail creates confusion rather than curiosity. Show enough context for viewers to know what they are waiting to understand.
three-act-short|Use three acts in a short narrated film|Three acts can organize a short film as a situation, a change and a consequence. They are a planning tool, not a requirement for equal-length sections or a rigid formula imposed on every subject.|Describe the starting situation in one line.~Choose the event that forces a decision.~End on the result of that decision rather than a general moral.|Outline a 60-second story: a watchmaker always repairs other people's clocks, discovers her own has stopped, and chooses to keep one hour free. Make the turning point visible through action. Use animation, English narration and a restrained ending.|Is something different after the middle?~Does the protagonist make a decision?~Is the final image a consequence?|An outline with three headings can still have no story. If the events could be rearranged without changing the meaning, strengthen the causal connection.
cause-and-effect|Make scenes connect through cause and effect|A sequence becomes easier to follow when each event changes what can happen next. Replace a list of attractive images with decisions and consequences that the viewer can connect.|Write because or therefore between consecutive scenes.~Identify any transition that only means and then.~Add the missing action or remove the disconnected scene.|Plan a 45-second animation about a gardener rescuing a dry seedling. The watering can is empty, so she collects rain in a cracked bowl; the bowl leaks, so she carries the seedling to the shelter instead. Keep the resolution connected to her decisions.|Why does each scene happen now?~Can the viewer infer the connection?~Does narration explain rather than conceal missing action?|Do not use narration to claim that one event caused another when the visuals show no relationship. Revise the storyboard before adding more explanatory words.
visual-payoff|Set up a visual payoff|A visual payoff gives an earlier object, action or composition new meaning. The audience needs to notice the setup without being told the ending in advance.|Choose one recurring visual element.~Introduce it clearly while it still has an ordinary purpose.~Change its context in the last scene to complete the idea.|In a 30-second animation, show a spare chair beside a solitary chess player. Return to the chair in the ending when a neighbor sits down. Keep the chair's color and position recognizable. Let the narrator say less at the final moment.|Was the setup visible?~Is the returning element recognizable?~Does its new context complete the story?|Adding an important object only in the last shot makes the ending feel arbitrary. Seed the object naturally and review its continuity across the rendered scenes.
nonfiction-story-arc|Build a factual explainer without invented drama|A factual story can follow a question, evidence and conclusion without inventing private conversations or personal motives. Organize verified material into a clear sequence and mark illustration as illustration.|Identify the question your sources actually answer.~Separate recorded events from your interpretation of their significance.~Use a visual explanation instead of a fictional quotation when evidence is limited.|Turn my supplied research notes into a 90-second explainer about a documented engineering repair. List the verified sequence first. Use generated illustrations rather than claiming to show the real event. Flag every proposed detail that is not present in the notes.|Are dates and causal claims supported?~Are reconstructions distinguished from records?~Does the conclusion match the evidence?|Dramatic dialogue can sound authentic even when invented. Do not put words in a real person's mouth to bridge gaps in your source material.
single-location-story|Tell a story in one location|A single-location story concentrates attention on changes inside a stable space. It can reduce unnecessary scene complexity, but the room still needs a meaningful action rather than a static description.|Define the space using two or three recognizable features.~Introduce a problem that can be resolved there.~Change framing and character action instead of adding new locations.|Plan a 45-second film inside a small fictional bookshop during rain. A bookseller tries to protect a leaking shelf, then discovers customers quietly helping. Keep the doorway, shelf and bucket in consistent positions. Use one narrator, with no spoken character dialogue.|Is the location understandable?~Do actions create progression?~Does the set remain consistent between shots?|One location is not a guarantee of visual continuity. Review furniture, entrances and prop positions, especially when different shots are generated separately.
object-led-story|Use an object as the center of a film|An object-led film follows a visible item's changing role. It works when the viewer can track the same object and understand what each encounter adds to its meaning.|Choose a distinctive but simple object.~Limit the number of owners or locations.~Give the final appearance a different emotional or practical role.|Write a 60-second narrated story about a yellow scarf left on a station bench. It first protects a fragile parcel and finally returns to its owner with a handwritten note added later in editing. Keep the scarf plain and recognizable in every shot.|Is the object identifiable?~Does every encounter change something?~Can the ending work without tiny generated lettering?|Highly intricate patterns make consistency harder to judge. Use a simple identifying feature and avoid making the plot depend on text that must be perfectly legible inside generated imagery.
parallel-storylines|Keep two storylines clear in a short video|Parallel storylines need distinct visual identities and an understandable reason to alternate. If the viewer spends the whole film identifying who is where, simplify to one thread.|Assign each thread a different location or recurring object.~Cut between them at matching story moments.~Bring them together through a shared event or explicit comparison.|Outline a 90-second fictional story alternating between a baker preparing bread and a ferry worker preparing a crossing. Use separate settings and one narrator. End when the bread reaches the ferry. Keep the timeline simple and explain why the two routines belong together.|Can each thread be recognized without labels?~Is their timing clear?~Does the connection justify the alternation?|Avoid adding a third storyline only to increase scale. A short running time leaves little room to recover from identity or timeline confusion.
ending-without-summary|End a film without repeating every point|An ending should complete the promise of the opening and leave one useful idea. Repeating the whole script can weaken a short film when the final image already communicates the result.|Return to the opening question.~Choose the single consequence that matters most.~Remove closing narration that merely describes an obvious image.|Revise the ending of a 45-second story about a repaired kite. Show the kite lifting and the maker releasing the string. End the narration on the choice to try again, without repeating the repair steps or adding a second motivational message.|Does the final image carry meaning?~Is the opening resolved?~Has a second ending been removed?|A sudden stop is not the same as a restrained ending. Give the viewer enough time to register the final action before the export ends.
episodic-video-series|Design a series with distinct episodes|A video series needs a repeatable promise and a different question in each episode. Reusing a visual structure can help recognition, but rephrasing the same answer does not create useful new episodes.|Write the series promise and its boundaries.~Give each episode a question that is not answered by its neighbors.~Keep a small list of recurring visual and narration choices.|Plan five short episodes on everyday repair decisions. Each episode should address a different decision, from diagnosing a loose part to knowing when to ask a professional. Return the episode questions and source needs first; do not generate repair instructions without verified material.|Does each episode add something new?~Can it stand alone?~Are references and recurring terms consistent?|Do not claim a process is safe merely because the animation is simple. Technical or hazardous advice needs appropriate sources and review before becoming a film.
storyboard-density|Choose how much happens in each scene|Scene density is the amount of new action and information a viewer must process at once. A brief becomes easier to render and review when each shot has one dominant event.|List the new idea introduced by each scene.~Split shots that combine several sequential actions.~Allow the narration to finish one thought before the visual introduces another.|Simplify a scene where a courier enters, reads a note, searches a room and repairs a radio. Make it three understandable beats: arrival, discovery, response. Keep the same overall story and tell me which details can be omitted in a 45-second version.|Can the dominant action be named?~Are important objects introduced before use?~Is a pause needed between ideas?|More shots do not automatically solve overload. If every cut introduces a new fact, reduce the information rather than accelerating the edit.
